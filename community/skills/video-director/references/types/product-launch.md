# Product launch film

A product or feature shown through its UI, motion graphics and sometimes people. Usually 30-90 s. The viewer should come away knowing what the product does for them and believing it, because they saw it work.

## Pick a structure

Choose by what the product's value looks like, and offer the user two of these as direction options when it isn't obvious.

**Continuous film (one space, one camera).** For a story of one person's work getting handled. The screen never resets; the camera moves through one 3D space (for example built as a few connected spaces), and one element turns into the next at every scene change. Beat order: the problem as a felt situation (a person, work piling up) → the product arrives, often carried by a color or chapter change → several product moments, each a real action with a result, with a different camera move under each change → the payoff (the same person, now handled, or the strongest proof) → close. Lines sit top-center about 9% down, the visual below, the bottom 17% empty; hard cuts only at one or two color or chapter changes, landed mid-motion.

**Chapter film.** For features that are separate abilities. Identity first (a mark, or something already moving within the first few seconds) → one promise, once, with a first light proof → 3-6 chapters, each a short claim and its proof → close. Chapter shares vary a lot (5-30% each); the strongest chapter often goes last. About 10-20 hard cuts are normal; a constant ground and a persistent actor keep them readable.
- A chapter can open on a short claim card: one outcome line of about 20-40 characters, dead center, held about 1-2.5 s, then a cut straight to the proof with no caption on it. This resets the screen, so ask the user before using it.
- A chapter can change to another flat brand color, with the UI on top. Layered depth in the UI is one option, not a requirement.
- The proof is already moving when the cut lands.

**One-task story** (example: Anthropic Cowork). For a product whose pitch is "give it a job": the setup (options, one picked, a request typed and sent) → the work (a clarifying question, a plan, steps ticking, the user steering) → close-ups of what was made → one wide view of the result → close.

**Day in the life** (example: Apple VoiceOver). For a feature whose benefit isn't visible on screen (voice, real-world, hardware): a tactile, wordless opening ritual → a few situations in the order of a day, with people and hands and the device as a prop → close. Text stays to a title and a closing name.

## Rules for every launch structure

- **One running example.** One fictional but specific project or task, with names, files and numbers that fit the audience, carried through the film.
- **Close-ups on what the claim is about; the whole window once.** UI text the viewer must read is at least 4-5% of frame height; full-window recordings at 1-2% text fail at phone size. Show the whole app window once, as the payoff, held long enough to take in (about 3 s).
- **People when the benefit is human or off screen.** Films whose benefit shows on screen often use no people; that is a choice, not a rule.
- **How the product is shown is a choice.** Offer it as the first look decision (stage 5), with 2-3 of these and what each costs:
  - **Recordings of the real screen** (Cursor 2.0). The most believable; full-window text is too small at phone size, so crop in close. Needs a working product and clean demo data.
  - **The real interface rebuilt to match it**, often cropped close (Cowork, Notion AI, Superhuman, Framer, Figma Motion, v0, Duolingo, Granola). The most common choice in the software launches studied. A buyer recognizes it from a demo. Needs the product's screens, Figma or a demo account; keep real words only where the viewer must read them.
  - **People or illustration with little or no interface** (Apple VoiceOver, Monzo). For a benefit that doesn't show on screen. The viewer doesn't learn what the product looks like.
  - Hardware: the physical product, filmed or rendered (Rivian R2, DJI Osmo Pocket 3, Nothing Phone (3), Dyson).
  Simplified made-up cards as the main view of the product appeared in none of the films studied: they look clean, but a buyer can't tell what the app looks like. If the user picks them, say that once and write the choice in the brief. Whatever is chosen, never show an invented result as if the product produced it.
- **Name the proof for each claim.** At the script stage, write beside every claim what on screen proves it, using the beat method in `../visual-ideas.md`: a counter, a highlighted row, a before and after, a result arriving. Example: a line saying other tools hit a limit gets a counter running down to 0. A row of cards moving past illustrates a line but doesn't prove it. At the references stage, note the moves worth borrowing (the camera moving through the UI, an outline on the row that matters, a literal counter), not only the timings.

## Text

- Continuous film: one line, top-center about 9% down, regular weight, the visual below it with clear space.
- Chapter film with claim cards: cards dead center (46-54% of the height); no caption over the proof.
- Product chrome (a prompt bar being used) may sit low in the frame; added text may not.

## Close

A closing line (about 3 s), then the logo alone (1-3 s): about 3-11 s in all, roughly 3-13% of the film. Never a two-sentence tagline (0 of 8 launches studied). Reuse the opening ground. The closing line is an invitation or a command, not a feature recap. Add a web address only when the viewer can act on a page now (1 of 8 studied: Superhuman); 6 of 8 end on the logo or wordmark, 2 on the product with no logo.

## Cursor and interaction

The cursor causes every change: it enters from off-frame, travels 0.7-0.8 s and slows into place, pauses, presses, the screen responds on release, then it slides off the text. The target shows its own press feedback; a ripple ring only if the user wants one. In a short or fast cut, shorten the travel.

## Logos of other tools

Show integrations as a stream of real logos (near ones fast, far ones slow) docking around the product, or as source names appearing in the product's own status line. Never a "+25 more" tile. Only integrations the product really has.

## Generated media in launch films

People and b-roll earn a place where the benefit is human (the person before and after) or not visible on screen. Generated media never shows UI, data or results. Keep generated hand and prop shots short.

## Examples seen in strong films (not rules)

- A product's own status line showing the "how" (sources scanned one at a time, steps ticking) instead of a logo wall (Notion AI, Cowork).
- One small brand mark that changes state when the product works, anchoring the eye across cuts (Notion's mascot, Cowork's star).
- The demo ending on a person typing a short, casual line into the product (Cowork).
- One flat comparison chart as the evidence beat, right after the claim (Cursor).

## Direction options differ by

The central picture idea (one person with the work arriving around them; one surface the camera glides across; one task from request to result; a day in someone's life), not three colors of one layout.

## Films worth studying

Superhuman (top-center line, people and marks as characters), Linear (one surface, one camera), Raycast (single large objects), Anthropic Cowork (one-task story), Notion AI and Cursor 2.0 (chapter films with claim cards), Figma Motion (chapter grounds), Framer (a live output as proof), Apple VoiceOver (day in the life). Watch for what carries across cuts and how the close is built.

## Default versus better

| Default | Better |
|---|---|
| A heading above each screenshot, one feature after another | One continuous space with a carried object; or a short claim, then the proof with no caption |
| The whole app window on screen the whole time | Close-ups on the one control the claim is about; one wide view at the payoff |
| Different fake data for each feature | One running task across the film |
| A logo wall for integrations | A stream of real logos docking, or source names in the product's status line |
| A tagline and a one-second logo | A closing line, then the logo alone |
| Simplified made-up cards standing in for the product | The real interface, recorded or rebuilt to match, cropped close |
| For a voice or real-world feature, animated UI | People and hands, the device as a prop |
