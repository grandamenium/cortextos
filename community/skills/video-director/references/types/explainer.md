# Explainer

A concept, process or "how it works" made visible with motion graphics, illustration and type, sometimes with footage or generated imagery. Usually narrated; 60-180 s. The viewer should be able to explain the idea to someone else afterward.

## Ask in the brief

- Narrated or captioned? Scene lengths come from the narration, so ask for the voiceover script (or write it) before fixing scene lengths.
- How the idea will be made visible (see below).
- Illustrated characters, hands only, or real-looking people? Simple drawn characters make a diverse cast easy and avoid uncanny faces.

## Structure (share of running time)

| Beat | Share | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | first 5-10% | A felt situation, a number, or a question already in motion; usually no logo and no title card |
| Name the idea | to about 35% | One picture of the idea or one metaphor, before any product or method |
| Mechanism | to about 70% | A few steps, each a new state of the same object or world, not a new layout |
| Payoff | to about 90% | The picture that shows the result: the widest, calmest shot of the film |
| Close | last 5-10% | A still card long enough to read the name, and the address if there is one |

Longer concept films (2 minutes or more) can add one-word chapter cards and one applied example before the close.

## Rules

1. **One object or one world carries the whole explanation.** The viewer recognizes it from the first scene to the last; each new idea is a change to it.
2. **Pick on purpose how the idea becomes visible.** Ways that work: a metaphor with a character, a diagram built one piece at a time (whiteboard or hand-drawn is one way to draw it; its rules are in `../visual-ideas.md`), one object transforming, a specimen shown as evidence with marks on it. When the idea has a shape (a hole, a collapse, a ladder), consider drawing that shape rather than an icon.
3. **The narrator carries the claim; the picture carries the thing; on-screen words carry only names, numbers and quoted evidence.** Never print the narration as on-screen lines.
4. **One sentence is one picture state.** Scene length follows the narrated sentence (3-6 s in the films measured). Long builds that aren't tied to one sentence keep their own length.
5. **Change the picture with the narration**, near the end of a phrase; reveal a piece when the voice names it.
6. **Speed follows the job**: fast for "everywhere", slower for "read this", longest for "follow this".
7. **An agent makes each change visible**: a hand, a finger, a character. It is the explainer's version of the cursor.
8. **A grid is allowed when the idea is "many"**, every cell alive and arriving in stagger.
9. Narration at about 2.5 words per second, one thought per sentence (`../sound-and-voice.md`).

## Text

- **Labels name the thing they sit beside**: 1-3 words, about 4% of frame height, with a leader line when needed. A section tag, if used, stays in one fixed place. Sentences are not printed.
- A title card is small and quiet, or absent.
- A spoken label stays on screen about as long as it is spoken; an unspoken one uses the reading time in `../text-and-captions.md` (set `narrated` per scene in the storyboard JSON).
- Color as a legend (a label colored like its object) is fine.

## Motion and transitions

- **Every cut or wipe carries something**: a foreground part becomes the next frame, an iris becomes the logo, a color flood becomes the next background, one line runs through a row of photos.
- Two families: **one world** (0-5 hard cuts; wipes, irises and morphs carry it) and **montage** (cuts every 1.5-2.5 s, held together by a thread: the same margin, type, color or line). Pick one.
- Diagrams and type hold still. Characters may keep small idle motion (blink, breath) during a hold.
- Depth is optional; most strong explainers are flat 2D.

## Color

Color marks state (the problem and the fix), category, or chapter, in the brand's own colors.

## Close

A still card of about 2-5 s: the logo, or one line, alone; never a two-sentence tagline (8 of 8 explainers studied). Add a web address only when the viewer can act on it now (2 of 8: a charity's own site, a sponsor). A film posted on a YouTube channel adds a 15-20 s end screen for YouTube's own end-screen links (Kurzgesagt, TED-Ed). The film's main shape closing into the logo is one option; silence is optional.

## Pictures, footage and generated images

- Most strong explainers use no photos at all. Generated images earn a place only when the explanation depends on what a real thing looks like (a specimen, a person, a place).
- Real documents (scans, forms, charts the film reads from) are built as layouts, never generated, so the words are right and can be marked.

## Examples seen in strong films (not rules)

- The problem shown as a situation rather than a statement: a traffic jam and a growing paper stack instead of the word "stress" (Headspace).
- A number that grows in front of the viewer beside something familiar for scale (Kurzgesagt).
- A photograph treated as a specimen: cut out on a flat field, labeled, the key part marked (Vox).
- Circles and underlines drawn on source documents (Vox).
- A very large single chapter word, about a fifth of the frame (Oddfellows).
- A deliberate empty frame when emptiness is the idea (Kurzgesagt's "zero dimensions"). This loosens the never-reset-to-empty rule: ask first.

## Direction options differ by

How the idea is made visible (character metaphor, built diagram, transforming object, marked specimen) and the world it lives in, not color.

## Films worth studying

Headspace "How It Works" (a character in a changing world), TED Countdown with Giant Ant "What is net-zero?" (the idea's shape as the picture), Kurzgesagt "How Small Is An Atom?" (one object at new scales), Vox "Why we say OK" (specimens with marks, a fast montage next to a slow build), Oddfellows "On Color" (one-word chapter cards).

## Default versus better

| Default | Better |
|---|---|
| Three steps as three slides with icons | One object that gathers the steps as it goes |
| Narration printed on screen | Narration carries the claim; words on screen are names, numbers and marked evidence |
| A wipe between unrelated pictures | A foreground part becomes the next frame; an iris becomes the logo |
| Even pacing, every scene the same length | Pace set by the sentence and the job: fast for volume, slow for mechanism |
| Brand colors as decoration | Color tied to state or category |
| A logo on a quick fade | A still card long enough to read |
